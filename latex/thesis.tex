\section{Introduction (2-3pg)}

In electrophysiology, two prevalent modes of biopotential measurements are electrocardiography (ECG) and electromyography (EMG), measuring cardiac muscle potentials and skeletal muscle potentials respectively. While methods that use intracellular electrodes exist and are much more precise, using surface electrodes is very common thanks to their non invasive nature, albeit at a loss of spatial specificity due to them being limited to measuring a superposition of nearby potentials. The field of ECG is by and large applied to identifying and diagnosing cardiac abnormalities in clinical and first responder settings, focusing on disturbances of the cardiac rhythm, artery blood flow, or electrolyte availability for diagnosis of heart conditions. On the other hand, Surface EMG (henceforth sEMG) has broader applications, ranging from the clinical, such as diagnosing nerve and muscle damage, to prosthetics, rehabilitation, biofeedback therapy, ergonomics, sports and movement science \cite{Barbero2012}, and it also opens up the possibility of novel Human-Computer Interaction methods.

Robot Operating System (ROS) is an open-source software framework for robotic applications, providing various mechanisms of interacting with hardware, but most importantly providing a flexible communication system ("the plumbing") that allows for real-time message-passing between networked nodes.\cite{ros} In practice, these nodes can be any component of the larger robotic system, be them anything from a discrete piece of hardware to a software component. Nodes communicate by sending messages, which can have varying levels of complexity, from primitives to nested aggregated structures, via "topics" which implement publish/subscribe semantics. In our application, the biopotential DAQ node can expose topics on which it publishes measured samples, for any other node to subscribe to for further signal processing.

The Linux Industrial I/O (IIO) subsystem was created in 2008 and mainlined in 2012 (\href{https://github.com/torvalds/linux/commit/06458e277eac2b8761b0a04d3c808d57be281a2e}{commit \texttt{06458e2}}) to provide a simple and unified way to interact with devices with ADC or DAC capabilities, broadly ranging from plain ADC/DAC chips to Inertial Measurement Units (Accelerometers, Gyroscopes, Magnetometers), Software Defined Radio, robotic actuators and sensors, and more. Within the Linux kernel, it fills in a gap between the \textit{input} subsystem which can stream user input data and the \textit{hwmon} subsystem used for monitoring and controlling hardware state such as CPU temperature and fan settings, albeit with low refresh rate. The IIO subsystem provides a modular way to define high bandwidth communication with input/output devices directly connected to the host via SPI or I2C and specified as part of the device tree. Despite the subsystem's name, it is not limited to industrial production applications: IIO usage is ubiquitous in today's linux-based devices for integrating features such as accelerometers, gyroscopes or ambient light sensors in a uniform way, besides other hardware abstraction layers.

\textit{Libiio} is a software library which provices cross-platform functionality for interacting with both local and remote IIO devices, within the same framework. To accomplish this, it implements the IIO Daemon (IIOD) for which it defines the \textit{IIOD protocol}. The current version of the IIOD protocol (v0.1) is based on a set of human readable commands sent via a single data stream of alternating client requests and server responses. This allows the same protocol to be used over a variety of transport layers, officially Serial (most relevant for this application), USB and TCP.

Although the electrical design of biopotential measurement devices has been studied for hundreds of years, tracing back to Luigi Galvani's work in the 18th century, and there exist a number of current-day commercial, academic, and DIY solutions, only a small part aspire to the ideals of Open Source Hardware and/or Open Source Software (ASPEN\cite{Esposito2018}, openBCI\cite{openbci}, BioAmp-EXG-Pill\cite{pill}, MyoWare EMG\cite{myoware}), and there exist no systems that integrate with ROS2 or the Linux IIO subsystem, limiting their flexibility in terms of building software that seamlessly interfaces with them.

We aim to fill this gap and provide an affordable and easily maintainable hardware and software solution for measuring multi-channel biopotential signals and interfacing them with ROS2 and LibIIO. Adapting this system to work with other software suites should be made as simple as possible thanks to the versatility of both ROS2 and LibIIO, and adapting it to other hardware platforms should also be facilitated by the decoupled nature of the hardware, firmware and software parts. An overview of the information flow, from the biological signals to the software, in figure \ref{fig:flow}.

Further on, we mainly focus on the electrical and firmware signal chain design and implementation, with lightweight but sufficient software wrappers for interacting with the final system through: libiio, a python library, and a ROS2 publisher node, respectively.

\subsection{Anatomical basis}

% (car), (ske), (pot)

As part of normal function, all biological cells have a difference of electrical potential between their interior and their exterior, called the \textit{membrane potential}. In the steady state, the \textit{resting membrane potential} of both skeletal muscle cells and that of cardiomyocytes (cardiac muscle cells) is in the $\numrange{-70}{-90} \mV$ range \cite{Hopkins2006,Klabunde2011-jo}.

\subsubsection*{Skeletal muscles: sEMG}

From an electrophysiological standpoint, skeletal muscles are comprised of a number of motor units (MU), each corresponding to an individual axon that innervates a number of muscle fibers. Neural impulses travel from the central nervous system via nerves to a motor unit's innervation zone (IZ), where a number of neuromuscular junction synapses trigger an action potential in the fibers of that motor unit. The electrical activation of the muscle doesn't take place all across the fiber simulataneously, but rather travels from the innervation zone towards the tendons as an impulse.

This starts out as an action potential (AP) at the innervation zone, wherein the span of 4-6ms, the membrane potential rises from the resting $-70 \mV$ to positive (depolarisation) and then back to the resting potential (repolarisation) by biomolecular mechanisms involving the flow of $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{K}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions. This localized action potential also triggers action potential in neighbouring parts of the muscle cell, leading to a chain reaction down the muscle fiber with a clear external electrical manifestation: the Motor Unit Action Potential as a traveling impulse of ion flow, which is measurable via the potential fluctuations across tissue.

Muscle fiber contractions follow the "all or nothing" principle, meaning a fiber either contracts fully or not at all. To modulate the force excerted by a muscle, only a part of the available motor units are recruited. Still, there is an independence between the number of motor units, number of fibers, and a muscle's strength, the motor unit count only improving the "resolution" of muscle control. By Henneman's size principle \cite{Henneman1957}, low effort contractions start by recruiting smaller motor units and gradually activate larger motor units as load increases. Besides this spatial control, muscular contraction is also controlled via rate coding, where the same motor unit is reactivated repeatedly, with the activation frequency positively correlating with the overall contraction strength, until fatigue is reached \cite{DeLuca1985}.

As a practical result, during low effort motion where only one or a handful of motor units are engaged, the APs are very easily identifiable in the measured waveform. On the other hand, high effort contractions will manifest a superposition of uncorrelated MUAPs which are difficult to separate from just sEMG measurements. (figure \ref{fig:apmuap})

Surface measurements have worse spatial resolution and cannot precisely pinpoint the source of the observed electrical activation, but rather "see" a potential halo. In the case of muscle fibers, the surface halo of a travelling action potential is modelled as a directional tripole with a leading positive depolarizing area, a trailing positive repolarizing area, and a strongly negative spike in the middle.\cite[Section 3]{Barbero2012}

\subsubsection*{Cardiac muscles: ECG}

While the internal mechanisms of muscle contractions are similar between cardiac and skeletal muscle, their external activation strategies vary almost entirely. Cardiac muscle isn't divided into motor units and as such heartbeat strength cannot be modulated by recruitment, but rather by various chemical and mechanical factors that ultimately increase or decrease the availability of $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ ions, such as but not limited to: the Frank-Starling mechanism (which aids in stroke volume regulation), or hormone effects from secretion of adrenaline or norepinephrine.

Contrasted to the usual sEMG measurement, all healthy heartbeats have a clearly identifiable structure consisting of a P wave, the QRS complex, and a T wave. (figure \ref{fig:ecg_pqrst}) Cardiac conditions can be characterized and diagnosed by these features' timing, but their electrical characteristics are very well defined, especially when compared to the noisy-looking MUAP superposition seen during skeletal muscle contraction.

\includesvg[width=0.4\linewidth]{ecg_pqrst}
\end{thesisfigure}

Another main difference between sEMG and ECG is electrode placement. While for sEMG we are interested in getting the electroces as close as possible to the muscle of interest to avoid confusion with other sources of electrical activity, ECG focuses on large scale electric field gradients that appear in the body as a result of a heartbeat. In essence, both the amplitude and "direction" of depolarization and repolarization can be determined by measuring potential differences across a patient's extremities. Localized contractions of cardiac muscle can be identified by the electric field disturbances they create even tens of centimeters away, bypassing the need to have electrodes as close as possible to the heart. All of this is possible in ECG but not EMG because of the very strong electrical impulses created by the heart, as well as their regular, non-superimposed structure. Various features of the heart's electrical activity can be identified by the directional characteristics of the potential induced in the body, making electrode placement very important for proper ECG.

Over time, conventional electrode layouts for ECG have been developed, aiming to measure well defined features of the heart's function. The standard ECG setup is the 12 lead system, in which 10 electrodes: RA (Right Arm), LA (Left Arm), RL (Right Leg, usually used as an active electrical reference through "Right Leg Drive"), LL (Left Leg), and the "precordial" V1-V6 electrodes placed in a line arrangement on the chest. From these, 12 electrical potentials (the "leads") are defined and used for ECG diagnosis:\cite{Meek2002}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Lead I, measured as the differential $\text{I} = LA - RA$
    \item Lead II, measured as the differential $\text{II} = LL - RA$ (coloquially recognized as "the" heart beat signal)
    \item Lead III, measured as the differential $\text{III} = LL - LA$
    \item Augmented vector Right, measured as the differential between $RA$ and the short-circuited $LA$ and $LL$: $\text{aVR} = RA - (LA+LL)/2$
    \item Augmented vector Left, measured as the differential between $LA$ and the short-circuited $RA$ and $LL$: $\text{aVL} = LA - (RA+LL)/2$
    \item Augmented vector Foot, measured as the differential between $LL$ and the short-circuited $RA$ and $LA$: $\text{aVF} = LL - (RA+LA)/2$ 
    \item 6 precordial leads (V1-V6, measured as single-ended signals (referenced to $RL$, for example, but not necessarily)
\end{itemize}

While the augmented leads' signals could be derived from the first three leads in an ideal system, noise considerations make it much more practical to short circuit the averaged leads and thus average their potentials by virtue of the created electrical voltage divider.

\subsection{Electronics for biopotential measurement}

In essence, biopotential measurements use mechanisms similar to any other electrical time series measurements, but extra design considerations must be taken into account because of the poor electrical characteristics of human bodies (high resistance, parasitic capacitance, easy coupling to nearby interference sources), as well as for patient safety.

\subsubsection{Biopotential measurement modes}

Both are relevant for a biopotential data acquisition system, and as such one of our goals is having a system that can do both.

\subsubsection{Driven Right Leg}

In both measurement modes, having the patient's body properly referred to a known electrical node is very important for the proper functioning of amplification and sensing circuitry. If the patient is "disconnected" from the rest of the circuit, and only interfaced to with high impedance sensing electrodes, stray charges originating from various sources can push the entire patient's body to common-mode potentials above or below the measurable range. Static electricity from clothing can regularly build up hundreds or thousands of volts, which easily exceeds the measurement range of common ADCs or amplifiers. While electrostatic discharge can be accounted for with protection circuitry, such high common-mode voltages make signals unrecoverable.

The solution is having a current path from the observed patient to the measurement aparatus' ground, through which charge imbalances can be equalized. This can just be a passive connection to ground (figure \ref{fig:drl} a), but using active components on this path allows us to modulate the potential difference between the patient and system ground, which opens up the opportunity to both bias all signals to a known voltage, as well as cancelling predictable or low-frequency noise, such as mains hum, by feeding its opposite as a bias.

For the practicality of such a biasing circuit, it must connect to the patient in a relatively far away part of the body from what is being measured, so it will minimally interfere with the other electrodes,  with as little introduced differential voltages as possible. As per ECG doctrine, the right leg is often used, leading to the name "Driven Right Leg circuit", but this referencing electrode can be connected anywhere depending on the measurement at hand.

\subsubsection{Electrode types}

The first step we control in the signal chain is the point of contact with the patient. The electrode type and quality have a great influence on the overall design, performance, and subjective user experience.

In the simplest case, just a metallic electrode connected to the body can sense electrical field changes, forming a polarizable electrode, which cannot offer consistent current flow, but rather acts like a capacitor, wherein ion concentrations on the skin surface will repel or attract electrons in the electrode, creating small currents.

A much more advantageous setup is one in which the physiological ion currents can be directly converted to electrical current akin to a resistive interface, termed a non-polarizable electrode. By far the most commonly used electrodes are Ag/AgCl electrodes which fit into this category.\cite{Lee2008} These have a silver chloride surface layer, backed by a silver core, and historically other metals for cost reduction. These are commonly distributed as both dry electrodes, which contact the skin directly, but require an electrolyte solution to be applied for best results, as well as wet electrodes, with the electrolyte solution suspended in an elastic gel that adheres to both the electrode and skin, both illustrated in figure \ref{fig:agcl_photos}. The electrolyte solution provides free $\text{Cl}^-$ ions that facilitate the conversion of electric current to ionic current by ionic decomposition of the $\text{AgCl}$ layer or recombining $\text{Cl}^-$ ions with $\text{Ag}^+$ ions. Dry electrodes can also somewhat function without any electrolyte solution applied thanks to the dissolved $\text{NaCl}$ content of sweat, which also provides free chlorine cations.

Chemically, the $\text{AgCl}$ layer partially decomposes into $\text{Ag}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions when exposed to the aqueous electrolyte solution. When the body potential is higher than the electrode potential, i.e. there's a lack of anions in the body, chlorine cations flow from the electrolyte gel towards the body, further encouraging the decomposition reaction $\text{AgCl} \rightarrow \text{Ag}^+ + \text{Cl}^-$ to replenish them. The positive $\text{Ag}^+$ can precipitate out on the electrode surface, exchanging an electron and thus inducing conventional electrical current "into" the electrode. On the other hand, when the body is more negatively charged than the electrode, $\text{Cl}^-$ ions in solution will be in excess and will recombine with $\text{Ag}^+$ ions on the $\text{AgCl}$ surface. The new lack of silver anions promotes the ionization of the metallic silver layer, releasing electrons. In both cases, the Ag/AgCl electrode and electrolyte solution act like a mostly resistive interface, with some stray parallel capacitance.

Because of their prevalence, Ag/AgCl electrodes are inexpensive and easily obtainable from general medical suppliers. Additionally, \cite{Lee2022} presents 3 methods for fabricating dry Ag/AgCl electrodes, which can be implemented even in small scales and using cheap materials, were these not commercially available.

The state of the art are conductive polymer PEDOT:PSS electrodes, thanks to their great mechanical, electrical, and chemical properties: They adhere and conform to skin, are flexible, self-healing, and have good biocompatibility, only falling behind the classic Ag/AgCl electrodes in terms of contact impedance, yet still being conductive enough for biopotential readings. They are not commonly available commercially. \cite{Seiti2023,Hou2024}

\subsection{Market research}

Biopotential measurements are no new subject and many products already exist both in the academic and commercial fields. All are subject to engineering tradeoffs between parameters such as number of channels, sample rate, precision, noise, electrode types, etc., making each better suited to a certain use case. To the best of our research, there is no universal, modular and extensible solution to the problem of biopotential measurements. Further on, we overview some representative options for EMG / ECG DAQ systems:

\subsubsection*{OpenBCI}

The OpenBCI ("Open Brain-Computer Interface") project started in 2013 and provides open source boards mainly intended for EEG measurements, but which are also applicable for EMG and ECG. These have underwent a handful of revisions in the last decade, and the current generation consists of:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cyton -- an 8 channel, 24 bit, DAQ based on the ADS1299. Retails for 917 Euro (as of 2024-08).
    \item Ganglion -- a 4 channel, 24 bit, DAQ based on the MCP3912. Retails for 458 Euro (as of 2024-08).
    \item Daisy -- an addon for the Cython providing 8 extra channels using a second ADS1299. Cython+Daisy package retails for 1835 Euro (as of 2024-08).
\end{itemize}

While the retail price on the OpenBCI store is steep, design files are provided for all products and they can be ordered and indivually built for a fraction of the cost. \subsection{Noise sources}

The superposition of electrical signals measurable by biopotential DAQs contains a complicated mix of noise coming from various physiological and other external factors, besides the signal of interest. Additionally, the measurement circuitry compounds noise at every step. Modeling some of the main noise components of the entire signal chain is essential for its design and proper specification.

Some of the studied noise sources are \textit{intrinsic} to the process and impossible to remove, such as thermal noise and the amplifier and ADC noise profiles. Others are not intrinsic, depend on the setup, and can be compensated for, such as mains hum.

\subsubsection{Thermal noise}

Even with ideal conductors and measurements, any resistive circuit will be affected by thermal noise, as a consequence of Planck's law of blackbody radiation \cite[chapter 3.2]{Urick2015-pm}. An ideal resistor $R$ at absolute temperature $T$ will manifest thermal noise $V_n$ over a frequency window $\Delta f$ having mean squared value \( \overline{V_n^2} = 4k_BTR\Delta f \), where $k_B = 1.380649\cdot10^{−23} \unit{J\per K}$ is the Boltzmann constant.

For a rough estimate of the order of magnitude of thermal noise in our biopotential measurements, we study the following hypothetical setup: Assume the electrodes attached to the patient are in thermal equilibrium at a typical skin temperature of $T \approx 308 \K$ \cite{Suarez2016}, we are limiting the frequency band to $\Delta f = 500 \Hz$ by filtering, and experiencing a typical resistance ${R \approx 3500 \ohm}$ (electrode + contact + internal resistance) \cite{Kurniawan2022}. This is only useful as a rough order estimation, because the resistance will vary depending on the electrode location, surface preparation, measured individual, electrode condition, etc. The estimated thermal noise will is:

\begin{thesisequation}{thnoisevalue}
\overline{V_n^2} = 4k_BTR\Delta f \approx 0.02977 \uV^2
\end{thesisequation}

Which can alternatively be interpreted as having an RMS noise of $\sqrt{0.02977 \uV^2} \approx 172.5 \nV$ or a noise density of $\sqrt{4k_BTR} = 7.716 \nV/\sqrt{\Hz}$.

\subsubsection{Amplifier and ADC noise} \label{ampandadcnoise}

Both ADCs and amplifier circuits have specified noise figures which pose design tradeoffs. In ADCs, the noise is specified as additive noise at the input $V_{na}$, such that a measurement $V_{meas}$ of a "real" voltage $V$ will be $V_{meas} = V + V_{na}$. Amplifiers are characterized by both input noise $V_{ni}$ created by the input stage, as well as output noise $V_{no}$ created by internal circuirty and the output stage. The output of an amplifier with gain $G$ is $V_{amp} = (V + V_{ni}) \cdot G + V_{no}$. As an easier to use figure, the \textit{Input Referred Noise} is the equivalent disturbance at the input that would create both the effects of input and output noise componded. $V_n = V_{ni} + V_{no}/G$ such that we can simplify to $V_{amp} = (V + V_n) \cdot G$. An equivalent block diagram is presented in figure \ref{fig:ampadcchain}.

This raises one of the most important design decisions of the signal chain, namely deciding on the amplification structure before the ADC. Using no amplifier at all is the most economic and also avoids $V_{ni}$ and $V_{no}$, but in the case of very small input signals, they are left to be dominated by ADC noise $V_{na}$. Adding an amplifier with unity gain only compounds amplifier noise and is as such detrimental, but further increasing the gain leads to better signal-to-noise ratio (SNR):

\begin{thesisequation}{snr}
\text{SNR} = \frac{\text{"signal"}}{\text{"noise"}} = \frac{G\cdot V}{G\cdot V_{ni} + V_{no} + V_{na}} = \frac{V}{V_{ni} + (V_{no} + V_{na}) / G} \longrightarrow \frac{V}{V_{ni}}
\end{thesisequation}

Thus, given an amplifier with known $V_{ni}$ we can compute the best-case noise figure assuming "infinite" gain. The rationally convergent nature of the above expression inevitably creates a situation of diminishing returns, wherein increasing the gain will always increase the signal quality, but even relatively small gains achieve practically ideal performance.

The drawback of having very large gain is the limit it incurs on the measurable range of input voltages. Any analog circuit can only handle a limited interval of voltages, which when exceeded leads to unreliable measurements, with the possibility of permanent damage to the equipment. If the ADC is limited to measuring $\pm V_{max}$, the measurable signals are implicitly limited to $\pm V_{max}/G$.


In this topology consisting of a single amplifier stage characterized by $V_{ni}$ and $V_{no}$, followed by an ADC with input noise $V_{na}$, measuring the "true" voltage $V$, the resulting digitized sample $V_{meas}$ can be decomposed into:

\begin{thesisequation}{decomposey}
V_{meas} = GV + GV_{ni} + V_{no} + V_{na}
\end{thesisequation}

Which can be rearranged to obtain an expression for RMS total noise $V_n$:

\begin{thesisequation}{rmsnoise}
\overline{V^2} = \frac{\overline{V_{meas}^2}}{G^2} + \underbrace{\left(\overline{V_{ni}^2} + \frac{\overline{V_{no}^2} + \overline{V_{na}^2}}{G^2}\right)}_{\overline{V_n^2}}
\end{thesisequation}

\subsubsection{Flicker noise}

Especially prevalent in BJT transistors and devices built using them, flicker noise is a low-frequency phenomenon where various material defects induce fluctuations with power density $P \propto 1/f$. It is also manifested in other active devices (MOSFETs, JFETs) and passive components, with a mix of various mechanisms contributing to it, many of which not fully agreed upon. A practical yet relatively complex solution to flicker noise is modulating the signal of interest to higher frequencies using a chopper and lock-in amplifier. \cite{flickernoise}

\subsubsection{ECG noise models}

Besides the previous intrinsic kinds of noise, further disturbances in ECG measurements can be classified, as per \cite{Chatterjee2020}, into: low frequency ($< 0.5\Hz$) baseline wander caused by body motion and electrode contact issues, power-line artifacts ($50/60\Hz$ and their harmonics) caused by insufficient power supply decoupling when the measurement device is connected to mains power and capacitive or inductive coupling between the patient and surrounding mains lines, and muscle artifacts (EMG signals). Of the three, the first two are relevant and undesireable in any biopotential measurement, while the latter is obviously the signal of interest in EMG applications.

From a signal processing standpoint, a simple solution for low frequency wander and power line artifacts is using a DC blocking comb filter, but these can also be addressed in the hardware design, especially when it comes to power supply decoupling.

\subsection{Signal chain design}

With today's available technology, signals in the millivolt range are relatively easy to measure even at quite high rates, if strongly driven. Intrusive methods of biopotential acquisition such as needle electrodes plugged directly into muscle fibers can work with no amplification stage, and just a direct connection to the ADC board, while still having good signal quality. On the other hand, when measuring with surface electrodes, both the electrode and biological tissue become a very high impedance element in the signal chain, which means that even relatively high biopotentials will have a hard time overcoming parasitic effects and signal chain noise before reaching the ADC.

\subsubsection{Instrumentation amplifiers}

Instrumentation amplifiers are a class of differential amplifier which boast high open-loop gain, low noise, and high input impedance. The typical textbook realization is with 3 operational amplifiers connected like in figure \ref{fig:inamp}, which allow the gain to be adjusted by choosing the value of resistor $R_G$. Their high input impedance means that very little current is needed to affect the inputs, helping with the issue of high electrode and tissue impedance. Their high power supply rejection ratio (PSRR) and common mode rejection ratio (CMRR) make them even more suitable for differential electrode applications, requiring very little extra circuitry to eliminate power line noise coming both from the electronics themselves, as well as coupled to the patient.

\subsubsection{Multiplexed ADCs}

Analog to Digital Converters come in a great variety of classes and specifications, depending on their intended use. The class most suitable for our use-case are multiplexed ADCs, which have a single internal measurement element, and multiplex one of many inputs to it to effectively do multiple channel time series measurements. The basic characteristics for selection include: the number of channels, output data rate (ODR), sample FIFO (or lack thereof), but for good signal-chain design, we must also carefully consider internal clock performance and independence from the serial clock, digital filtering capabilities, channel multiplexing dead time and other noise characteristics. We explain each of these further.

\textbf{The number of channels} and \textbf{ODR} are self explanatory, directly impacting the characteristics of the resulting time series.

Some multiplexed ADCs have internal \textbf{sample FIFOs} which can accumulate measured samples and which allow the host to read multiple queued up measurements instead of having to poll each sample as it becomes available. While it does not have an effect on signal quality, having an internal FIFO relaxes firmware timing constraings.

Many multiplexed ADCs come with \textbf{integrated digital filters}, often not bypassable. These help with signal quality and are typically used for rejecting unwanted frequency bands, especially powerline noise. While they increase single-channel performance, they often introduce \textbf{multiplexing dead time}, wherein a filter must process a number of useless samples to enter its steady state regime after a channel switch. This dead time can greatly reduce overall data rate to even orders of magnitude less than the maximum data rate when iterating through all channels.

Sample acquisition timing is essential and has great impact on the resulting signal. Clock jitter manifests itself as phase noise due to aperture uncertainty, limiting SNR according to equation \ref{eq:jitternoise}\cite{AN-501}, where $f$ represents signal frequency and $t_a$ represents RMS clock jitter in seconds-RMS.

\begin{thesisequation}{jitternoise}
\text{SNR} < -20\log(2\pi f t_a)
\end{thesisequation}

This phenomenon eliminates ADCs which depend on the serial clock for their internal logic, such as the AD5592 and AD5593, adding a strict requirement for independence between the communication clock and internal ADC logic clock.

Finally, various internal phenomena introduce noise dependent on the measurement configuration, with a typical increase of noise with increasing measurement rate. Datasheet-specified noise figures should be consulted for the chosen parts.

\subsubsection{EMI prevention over long wires}

When dealing with long wires and weak signals, electromagnetic interference (EMI) is a significant source of noise which is produced by virtually all electronic devices around. To combat this, common techniques are using twisted pairs and wire shielding. For simplicity, we will try to shorten the wires carrying weak signals as much as possible and only use strongly driven and high amplitude signals over long wires. The result is that in bipolar measurements, we place the differential amplifier as close as possible to the electrodes, and then don't care about signal degradation between the amplifier and ADC thanks to the strongly driven nature of that signal.

\subsubsection{Interfacing with the patient}

Taking from the research from the previous electrode type research, we do not consider PEDOT:PSS electrodes to be viable for this project due to them not being commercially available in standard and mass produced forms, being mostly a custom product experimented with in today's laboratories, even half a century after their discovery in the 1980s.

We settle for for snap-on adhesive Ag/AgCl gel electrodes thanks to being cheap, readily avaialble, standardized, and they can be connected to with inexpensive aligator clips, besides special (but more expensive) snap clips. The downsides are their comparatively larger contact area (cca $200\mm^2$ vs \cite{Barbero2012}'s 2mm electrode diameters), and are lacking in subjective user experience, sometimes being very painful to detach.

\subsubsection{ADC}

We initially chose the AD7124-8\cite{AD7124-8} thanks to its very diverse feature set, including: programmable gain amplifiers (PGAs) which had the potential to remove the need for the external amplification stage, high precision, high number of channels, and high sample rate. Unfortunately, it is not a single cycle ADC. This means that although it has very good throughput on a single channel, when switching between two channels there is a dead time for the integrated filter to settle, which effectively reduces the per-channel sample rate to less than $100\Hz$ when sampling all 16, which is virtually useless for ECG ($f_\text{max}=150\Hz$) and EMG ($f_\text{max}=500\Hz$).

Our next choice was the AD4114\cite{AD4114}. It doesn't include PGAs, but otherwise has very good specificationss and has a much higher sample rate and a single cycle conversion mode. Switching ADCs was facilitated by the modular nature of the project.

Moreso, to avoid the need for PCB design, we use the EVAL-AD4114SDZ evaluation board, which already implements isolation between the digital and analog sides, overvoltage and ESD protection, an external voltage reference, and various other features.

The AD717X family of ADCs, which the AD4114 is part of (in spite of the numbering scheme), all expose a similar interface to the application developer. A number of channels can be enabled, which are then measured in a loop, in ascending order of their indices, by use of an internal sequencer. Each of the 16 channels can select one of 34 inputs: 16 unipolar measurements ($V_{in,k} - V_{incom}$, 16 bipolar measurements ($V_{in,2k} - V_{in,2k+1}$ and $V_{in,2k+1} - V_{in,2k}$), an internal temperature sensor or the voltage reference itself. If an input is selected by multiple channels, it is read multiple times in the sequence. To parametrize a channel's conversion, one of 8 "setups" can be assigned. Setups describe whether a measurement is unipolar of bipolar, the used reference, buffering and filtering settings. Each setup has a corresponding digital filter with an internal state. If multiple channels refer to the same setup, they will also be alternately using the same digital filter, requiring some extra logic for avoiding latent (i.e. "hidden") samples from passing from one channel's to another's readouts. Conveniently, the \texttt{SING\_CYC} flag puts the ADC in "single cycle mode", which always flushes out the digital filter when switching channels, at the expense of worse filter performance, data rates, and greater noise.

The AD4114 lacks an internal sample FIFO, meaning that data from a conversion must be read before the next one or else it is lost. While the digital interface allows for an interrupt signal on the \drdy line to notify the host of new data, it is incompatible with most microcontrollers' hardware SPI implementations and it is much more suitable for an FPGA host. Therefore we can and should resort to polling the status register to check for new samples, which are announced through the \texttt{RDY} bit. Polling should be done at least as fast as the conversion rate. Experimentally, we deduced a $5\MHz$ limit for SPI communication, in spite of the theoretical $20\MHz$ limit of the AD4114, likely due to parasitic effects on uneven length jumper wires for high speed SPI.

One of the most important limitations in determining which parameters to run the ADC in is its noise, which varies with the sample rate. While high sample rates are very desirable given we have practically limitless digital signal processing capacity, they also degrade the signal quality. This is especially relevant when sampling multiple channels, where the Output Data Rate (ODR) is divided between them. A single channel measurement with $\ge 500\Hz$ bandwidth can be done with the $1007\Hz$ setting for an effective resolution of $17.3\text{ bits}$, but having 6 channels forces the use of the $6211\Hz$ setting with $15.4\text{ bits}$ of resolution, and more channels than that start limiting the bandwidth to less than $500\Hz$.

For this purpose, we selected 7 configurations depending on the number of required channels so as to maintain at least $1000\Hz$ per-channel sample rate (table \ref{tab:configs}). Measurements of up to $6$ channels (single-ended or differential alike) can use the better performing sinc5+sinc1 filter. Measurements of $7 - 10$ channels cannot achieve the desired sample rate without switching to the faster yet more noisy sinc3 filter. Measurements of $11 - 16$ channels cannot achieve the desired sample rate in any configuration using the AD4114, with the max per-channel sample rate degrading to $10309/16=644.3125\Hz$ for the maximum of 16 channels.

\subsubsection{Amplification stage}

The chosen AD4114 ADC has $N=24$ bits of resolution with one LSB equating to the following in the bipolar measurement mode.
\begin{thesisequation}{lsb}
\text{LSB} = V_{REF} / (2^{N-1} \cdot 0.1)
\end{thesisequation}

Using the internal $2.5\V$ reference, $\text{LSB} \approx 2.98\uV$. The noise level varies depending on the sample rate. At the single-channel $f_s=1007\Hz$, 6 channel $f_s=6211\Hz$, and all channel $f_s=10309\Hz$, the ADC noise is specified to be around $\overline{V_{na,1007}^2}=20\uV$, $\overline{V_{na,6211}^2}=74\uV$ and $\overline{V_{na,10309}^2}=1068\uV$ RMS respectively. Without an amplification stage, this would result in the following SNRs relative to a $1\mV$ signal typical of ECG/EMG:

\begin{thesisequation}{SNR_no_amp}
\begin{aligned}
\text{SNR}_{(\dB)} &= 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{(1\mV)^2}{\overline{V_{na}^2}}\right) \\
\text{SNR}_{1007} &\approx 34\dB \\
\text{SNR}_{6211} &\approx 23\dB \\
\text{SNR}_{10309} &\approx -0.6\dB
\end{aligned}
\end{thesisequation}

In the case of 7+ channels, a $1\mV$ signal slightly dips under the noise floor, becoming unmeasurable. Thus, a preamplification stage is required for enabling the use of all channels.

We choose the AD8226 \cite{AD8226} instrumentation amplifier as the central component of the preamplification stage. It is an inexpensive, readily available instrumentation amplifier with more than sufficient CMRR ($>80\dB$). It can be configured for a gain of $G=\numrange{1}{1000}$ using a resistor $R_G = 49.4\kohm / (G-1)$. Its RMS voltage noise for our desired bandwidth of $\Delta f=500\Hz$ is:

\begin{thesisequation}{8266_noise}
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{\overline{V_{ni}^2}} = \sqrt{500\Hz \cdot (22\nV/\sqrt{\Hz})^2} = 0.492\uV \\
\sqrt{\overline{V_{no}^2}} = \sqrt{500\Hz \cdot (120\nV/\sqrt{\Hz})^2} = 2.683\uV
\end{aligned}
\end{thesisequation}

With the preamp stage added before the ADC, the total noise of a sample and the theoretical SNR are:

\begin{thesisequation}{noise_amp}
\begin{aligned}
\text{SNR}_{(\dB)} &= 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{(1\mV)^2}{\overline{V_{n}^2}}\right) \\
(\ref{eq:rmsnoise}) \Rightarrow \text{SNR}_{(\dB)} &= 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{(1\mV)^2\cdot G^2}{\overline{V_{ni}^2}\cdot G^2 + \overline{V_{no}^2} + \overline{V_{na}^2}}\right)
\end{aligned}
\end{thesisequation}

As described in section \ref{ampandadcnoise}, increasing the gain will increase SNR, but with diminishing returns, and it will limit the available measurement range. For the AD8226, we compute a handful of gain scenarios (table \ref{tab:gains}) and choose $G\approx 20$ as a fair middleground between good SNR and good input range, allowing for some fluctuations without saturating the sensors.

We use the part's gain formula to compute a suitable gain resistor (equation \ref{eq:gainres}). The closest E6-series value is $R_G = 2200\ohm$ which would give a gain of $G\approx 22.45 \approx +27\dB$.

\begin{thesisequation}{gainres}

\end{thesisequation}

Using an active element (the instrumentation amplifier) also gives us the opportunity to do some hardware filtering. To combat high frequency noise, we add a first order RC low-pass filter with a cutoff of $f_c \approx 500\Hz$. Our implementation uses $R=330\ohm$ and $C=1\uF$ for a theoretical $3\dB$ cutoff frequency of:

$$f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi \cdot 330\ohm \cdot 1\uF} = 482.28\Hz$$

To encode the bipolar differential measurement as a unipolar signal, we bias the amplifier's output voltage using the AD8226's $V_{REF}$ input. To generate the bias voltage, we use a $10\kohm - 10\kohm$ voltage divider from $5\V$ to ground, resulting in $2.5\V$. As specified in the datasheet, this pin must be driven with less than $2\ohm$ impedance, so this wouldn't suffice. We use a common-collector voltage buffer to amplify the divider's drive strength, biasing the output to approximately $V_{REF} \approx 5\V / 2 - 0.7\V = 1.8V$ after the PN junction voltage drop. To help with transient power supply fluctuations, the instrumentation amplifier's positive supply as well as $V_{REF}$ are decoupled with $0.1\uF$ capacitors. The second should also reduce BJT flicker noise coming from the voltage buffer.

\subsection{Firmware part}

For the firmware, we will be structuring our code around No-OS\cite{noos}, an open-source firmware framework developed by Analog Devices that defines a common API for accessing common peripherals on embedded systems. While its scope somewhat overlaps with a typical Hardware Abstraction Layer (HAL) within an Operating System, no-os focuses on providing minimal abstractions to run directly on bare metal, as the name suggests. Additionally, it provides abstractions for the platform architecture, build systems and custom vendor SDKs, such that code can be very easily ported from one platform to another.

The benefits of using such a versatile framework were constantly apparent during this project's development, which required us to change the microcontroller platform, peripherals, as well as communication protocols during protoyping. While each change required small rewrites of the codebase, the uniform structure of no-os meant we didn't have to rewrite thousands of lines of code after each hardware change.

\subsubsection{Serial communication with host}

In order to send data to the attached computer or carrier board, the de facto solution is using UART-over-USB, which exposes an USB interface to the attached host and UART to the microcontroller. Most commonly, microcontrollers have dedicated circuitry for UART transmit and receive, which allows the rest of the program to run concurrently to serial communication. This is implemented using two FIFO buffers, one for data pending to be sent from the microcontroller (TX FIFO) and one for received data ready to be processed by the program (RX FIFO).

De facto, most microcontroller development boards, including the MAX78000FTHR, and the more common ATMEGA368P "Arduino-like" boards provide UART-USB translation via a dedicated IC, abstracting away the complexities of the USB communication stack.

UART modules have varying degrees of configurability of the baud rate, flow control, and framing. For most practical uses, though, including this project, one may just specify the baud rate, and default to 8N1 (8 data bits, no parity bit, 1 start bit, 1 stop bit) framing and no flow control. Typical baud rates include 9600 baud, a legacy of 20th century POTS modems (introduced by the V.32 ITU-T standard) and 115200 baud, common both in last-century modem systems, but also recently popularized by its default use in the Arduino development environment.

To achieve the required bandwidth for this project, a suitable baud rate must be chosen. Given the output data rate $\text{ODR}$, number of bytes per channel sample $d$, and 8N1 framing (giving $10\text{ baud}/\text{byte}$), the baud rate $f_{baud}$ must be:

\begin{thesisequation}{baud}
f_{baud} \geq \text{ODR} \cdot d \cdot 10 = 1024 \cdot 10309 \cdot 4 \cdot 10 = 412360\text{ baud}
\end{thesisequation}

Protocol overhead means we must choose a baud rate safely above it. Choosing a baud rate larger than this minimum will also allow for less timing concerns, especially when it comes to not overflowing the receive and transmit buffers.

Baud rate selection is not universal across devices and the firmware for each hardware platform must choose a rate that is both compatible with the hardware and that satisfies relation \ref{eq:baud}. In the case of the MAX78000FTHR, a rate of exactly $491520\text{ baud}$ can be achievend by using the IBRO (Internal Baud Rate Oscillator) of $7.3728\text{MHz}$ with a clock divider of $15$, but we will use a rounder $500000\text{ baud}$ for convenience.

\subsubsection{LibIIO integration}

It is quite common for no-OS projects to provide IIO functionality, and for this reason no-OS has a dedicated LibIIO protocol implementation. This provides a convenient way of describing LibIIO contexts, with customizable callbacks for all read/write operations with both actual sample data and attributes. Additionally, it implements an internal event loop which abstracts away asynchronous communication with both the host computer and the connected peripherals, which is a major concern in a system where concurrent real-time communication takes place.

The first step is defining the structure of the IIO context is, as it solidifies a mapping between the device-specific functionality on the hardware side and the application-specific functionality on the software side. There is a spectrum of choice between exposing attributes for very low-level details (such as individual flags in registers), which offloads most application logic to the software side, and exposing more high-level attributes which are translated into register values by the firmware. Because we are not defining a general-purpose ADC device, we will restrict ourselves to only one configurable attribute per channel, namely the desired input to measure, opaquely choosing the best suited sample rates, filter settings, and setup assignment based on table \ref{tab:configs}. Read-only attributes will expose the computed sampling frequency for the device, as well as calibration values (scale and offset) and a list of available input pairs for each channel.

De facto, attributes with values selectable among a set (such as the channel input pair selection) are named by appending \texttt{\_available} to the name of the attribute they refer to. In our case, channels have a read/write attribute \texttt{input} for querying or setting the selected input pair, as well as the read-only \texttt{input\_available} which contains a space-separated string of all input pair names.

For lower-level functionality and debugging, an IIO debug interface is also specified, which allows for direct manipulation of the ADC's registers. This shouldn't be normally used, but allows for total control of the device in case the user needs it.

Besides setting up the IIO device, we must also initialize an "IIO app", which is the daemon itself, which may communicate with multiple devices. The IIO app is initialized through the \texttt{iio\_app\_init\_param} struct passed to the \texttt{iio\_app\_init} function, which contains: a list of all devices, UART parameters, a list of hardware triggers.

We must also allocatea buffer for samples to be stored in between them being submitted by the ADC and them being read by the host computer. In our implementation, this is a static fixed-size buffer in the global namespace, with room for $1024$ samples on $16$ channels, for around one second buffer time:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C]
char _iio_ad4114_samples_buf[16 * 4 * 1024];
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection{Patching platform drivers for fast UART}

One issue encountered with our setup, which is specific for no-OS IIOD on maxim microcontrollers, was the very slow implementation of assynchronous UART receive operations. This is due to a high amount of indirection, wherein both the underlying Maxim SDK (msdk), the no-OS MAX78000 platform driver, and the no-OS IIOD implementation use software implementations for interrupt handling with an excess of function pointer tables, dynamic memory allocations, and unnecessary control structures, all of which slowed down UART communication enough for it to break the high throughput we need. The main symptom was that an UART byte receive interrupt would take so long to execute that the next byte would arrive before it is done, entering a nested interrupt thanks to the NVIC (Nested Vector Interrupt Controller) and breaking application logic.

Prior to our modification, the function call chain upon receiving a byte via UART would be: \texttt{UART0\_IRQHandler}, \texttt{\_uart\_common\_handler}, \texttt{MXC\_UART\_AsyncHandler},\\\texttt{MXC\_UART\_RevA\_TransactionAsync} which queues a callback in no-os which ultimately ends up calling \texttt{uart\_rx\_callback}, \texttt{max\_uart\_read\_nonblocking} which indirectly calls\\\texttt{max\_uart\_callback} which is the end of the chain. Needless to say, this is an extreme amount of overhead for what is essentially passing an 8 bit value from the UART receive register to the no-OS UART read buffer.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C]
void UART0_IRQHandler()
{
    _uart_common_handler(MXC_UART0);
}
\end{lstlisting}

We propose a lightweight modification which bypasses this entire mechanism and replaces all of it with just a very quick 8 bit store to a circular buffer. In case error bits are set in the UART registers, we fall back to the original implementation, which handles edge cases. For storing queued bytes, we use a \texttt{lf256fifo}, a no-OS utility structure for a very efficient 256 byte circular buffer.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C]
void UART0_IRQHandler()
{
    // Short circuit complex logic
    bool fallback = true;
    if(g_uart0_rx_fifo &&
        (MXC_UART0->int_fl & MXC_F_UART_INT_FL_RX_THD))
    {
        // Got one byte. Clear interrupt:
        MXC_UART0->int_fl |= MXC_F_UART_INT_FL_RX_THD;
        // Store byte in fifo
        lf256fifo_write(g_uart0_rx_fifo, MXC_UART0->fifo);
        fallback = false;
    }

    // fall back to slow handler if there was an unexpected state:
    if(fallback)
        _uart_common_handler(MXC_UART0);
}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection{Continuous and asynchronous data acquisition using IIO triggers}

By default, the expected behaviour of IIO devices is to start acquisition on selected channels when requested via an \texttt{OPEN} command, and stop and disable the channels once the requested number of samples is reached. In low sample rate scenarios, the host may request the next acquisition window very soon after the current one finishes, and assuming the time delay between windows can be minimized with respect to the overall window size, this could prevent missing any samples and only introduce a small amount of phase drift. Additionally, there are high-speed applications in which continuity between acquisition windows is not strictly required. On the other hand, with our roughly $10\kHz$ sample rate (cca. $100\us/\text{sample}$) and requirement to not miss any samples, this application has very little room for error in timing. As per \cite{linuxlatency}, modern real-time linux setups have scheduler latencies in the tens and hundreds of microseconds, which realistically leaves no time to actually receive the current window's buffer and send a new acquisition request without missing any samples, even if the acquisition channels were left enabled.

With this general issue in mind, IIO defines triggers as software or hardware events that initiate data acquisition without requiring regular polling from the host. Data captured as a result of triggers is stored in circular buffers and provided with minimal latency whenever the host polls next.

With the AD4114 in continuous conversion mode, two trigger sources are available: using the \drdy signal to get an interrupt when a new sample is ready, or using a timer to poll the AD4114 at least as fast as the conversion rate. While the first option is more elegant and wouldn't do any unnecessary polling, it requires nonstandard operation of the SPI protocol by keeping CS active and monitoring the MISO (\drdy) line, which isn't generally possible unless we implement the SPI protocol in software or have access to more configurable IO options like on FPGA platforms or the Programmable Input and Output subsystem of RP2040 fame (\cite{Smith2021}). The second option of setting up a timer interrupt which polls the AD4114 status register to check if a new conversion result is available and easy to implement.

To compute a suitable interval for the polling frequency $f_p$ it is trivial that $f_p \geq \text{ODR} = 10309\Hz$. Additionally, we cannot poll faster than it takes to do the polling itself, which is an SPI transfer of 2 bytes (1 request byte, 1 response byte with the status register), amounting to $T = 2 \cdot 10 / f_{SPI} = 4\us$ and limiting $f_p \leq 250\kHz$. There is no use in polling orders of magnitude faster than samples become available, so we settle for polling at around $1.5\times$ the sampling ratio, with $f_p = 16000\Hz$. In the highest data rate scenario, we will have a sample available every $16000/10309 \approx 1.55$ polls on average. In the lowest data rate scenario, this number rises to $16000/1007 \approx 15.89$ polls per sample.

The regularly timed polling is done by using an IIO hardware trigger based on a timer IRQ. When any channels are active, the timer IRQ is enabled by the underlying IIO implementation and its handler can poll, get data, and store it in a buffer to be sent to the host later. Besides the time required for communication, storing the samples should also be done as quickly as possible to not take up microcontroller execution time. In our implementation, this is done by just a single 32 bit store in a static sample buffer. When the sample buffer is full (i.e. all channels have stored one value), it is submitted to the no-OS IIO implementation via a call to \texttt{iio\_buffer\_push\_scan}. A shortened implementation of the trigger / IRQ handler follows:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C]
trigger_handler(struct iio_device_data *dd)
{
    // 2 byte SPI transfer to poll if a sample is ready
    int ret = check_ad4114_ready(dev);
    if(ret == -EAGAIN) return 0;

    AD717X_ReadData(adc, &data);

    // Data contains status register as well:
    int8_t status = data & 0xff;
    int8_t channel = status & 0xf;
    data = data >> 8; // actual measurement is only the first 3 bytes

    dev->sample_buf[dev->channel_offset[channel]] = data;
    
    // Got a full sample of all channels
    if(channel == dev->last_enabled_channel)
        iio_buffer_push_scan(dd->buffer, dev->sample_buf);
}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection{Python wrapper class}

For programatic interaction with the device, we create a Python 3 package defining a device class that exposes functionality for connecting to a device, configuring its channels and starting acquisition, receiving samples, and stopping acquisition.

We extend the \texttt{pyadi-iio} library, which is itself a wrapper around the \texttt{pylibiio} bindings for \texttt{libiio}. While the bindings themselves would suffice, they expose low level libiio details which are unnecessary for this project. An example snippet of how this package can be used is presented:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
from ad4114exg_iio import ad4114exg_iio

# Connect to libIIO device based on URI
uri = 'serial:/dev/ttyACM0,500000'
dev = ad4114exg_iio(uri)

# Start acquisition of the following inputs
dev.start(['VIN13-VINCOM', 'VIN2-VIN3', 'VIN3-VIN2'])

# Query sample rate, based on the number of inputs
fs = dev.sample_rate
print(fs)

# Receive 100 windows of measured data and print
for i in range(100):
    window = dev.rx()
    print(window)
\end{lstlisting}

Within \texttt{pyadi-iio}, common ADC and DAC functionality is clustered into the \texttt{tx}, \texttt{rx}, and the merged \texttt{tx\_rx} classes. Additionally, connecting to a LibIIO context is done via the \texttt{context\_manager} class. Taking from other ADC implementations, we define a device class inheriting from \texttt{rx} and \texttt{context\_manager}. 

The most relevant library functions we use are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{\_set\_iio\_attr} and \texttt{\_get\_iio\_attr} for reading and writing device and channel attributes, such as the selected inputs
    \item \texttt{rx\_buffer\_size} for controlling the size of received buffers. Larger buffer sizes result in rarer and bulkier transfers (within the limits of the firmware buffer), and smaller buffer sizes result in having transfers happen more often with smaller payloads. We set a size of $16$ samples / transfer for an estimated $1000\Hz / 16 \approx 60\Hz$ buffer transferrate.
    \item \texttt{rx\_enabled\_channels} selects enabled channels and starts data acquisition upon being written to
\end{itemize}

Overall, given the amount of logic implemented "behind the scenes" by \texttt{pyadi-iio}, our final device implementation is mostly boilerplate code.

\subsubsection{ROS2 publisher node}

The chosen data format for biopotential measurements is a timestamped float32, defined as the message \texttt{ExgSample.msg}:

\begin{lstlisting}
std_msgs/Header header
float32 value
\end{lstlisting}

While the header field adds a small size overhead, it helps with consistency and interoperability with other ROS2 projects, it being the de facto standard to timestamp messages. For instance, plotjuggler assumes timestamped data to have a \texttt{header.stamp} field.

Because of Docker limitations, we cannot easily and reliably communicate with the device via serial from within the container. Thankfully, libiio's transport layer agnostic nature means we can still make a connection between a ROS2 node and the device by launching iio daemon on the host and connecting to it from the container over TCP.

Building on top of the libiio wrapper class, we develop a ROS2 node that communicates with the iio daemon, buffering incoming data, and regularly publishes it to the ROS2 network. Because these two are implicitly asynchronous and may happen at differing rates, they must be separated into two threads, communicating via a synchronized FIFO.

The Python 3 standard library offers very easy to use functionality for this: the threading library allows for one-liner instantiation of the iio thread, and the queue library implements a thread-safe FIFO, removing the need for hand-implemented thread synchronization mechanisms.

The main thread runs the rclpy loop and publishes all queued up samples at a rate of $1\kHz$:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
class AD4114ExgPublisher(Node):
    #[...snip...]
    def __init__(self, uri, channels):
        #[...snip...]
        self.buffer = Queue()
        self.timer = self.create_timer(1e-3, self.publish_buffered_samples)
        #[...snip...]

    def publish_buffered_samples(self):
        while not self.buffer.empty():
            (timestamp, channel, value) = self.buffer.get()

            msg = ExgSample()
            msg.header.stamp = Time(seconds=timestamp).to_msg()
            msg.value = value
            self.publisherz[channel].publish(msg)
\end{lstlisting}

Concurrently, the iio thread connects to the iio daemon, receives samples in blocks, assigns linearly interpolated timestamps, and pushes them to the common buffer:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
def receive_thread(self, channels):
    self.iiodev.start(channels)

    t0 = time()
    while True:
        data = self.iiodev.rx()
        t1 = time()
        
        N = data[0].size
        for i, chan in enumerate(channels):
            for j, value in enumerate(data[i]):
                value = (value - 2**23) * 3e-6
                t = t0 + (t1 - t0) * j / N
                self.buffer.put((t, i, value))

        t0 = t1
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Results and validation}

\subsection*{Preamplifier module}

We evaluate the designed preamplifier module's performance using two tests based on the ADALM2000 platform, which powers the module, generates test signals, and measures the amplified output.

For the first test, a sinusoidal $A_{test} = 50\mV$ peak-peak test signal is applied. The measured waveform displays the expected DC bias of $\overbar{V_{amp}}=1.88\V$, and an amplitude of $A_{amp}=1.23\V$ peak-peak, giving an effective gain of $G = A_{amp} / A_{test} = 24.6$, just within the $10\%$ tolerance of the designed $G=22.45$, justifiable by the tolerances of the passive components we used. For these amplitudes, the harmonic distortion and amplifier noise are below the $-80\dB\text{FS}$ noise floor of the ADALM2000.

Secondly, we test the frequency response using the Network analyzer feature of the Scopy software\cite{scopy}. A $10\Hz - 10\kHz$ sine sweep is generated for the amplifier's input, and the resulting output is measured, determining both the magnitude and phase relation, which are plotted in figure \ref{fig:preampbode}. Both the measured magnitude and phase characteristics match the ideal frequency response well within the $10\%$ tolerances of the passives. For frequencies above $1\kHz$, the signal amplitude decreases to small multiples of the ADALM2000 resolution, leading to increasing amounts of quantization noise, which can be seen in both the magnitude plot as an increased reported magnitude, as well as in the phase plot as phase noise. This isn't concerning, as it is a limitation of the ADALM2000 used for the test setup, not the preamplifier board itself. More importantly, the desired amplification and corner frequency are validated.

\subsection*{Multi-channel EMG}

With EMG signals above the circa $1\mV$ noise threshold of the max data rate conversions, measuring 16 single-ended channels can be done without using 16 preamplifier boards, albeit at a loss of signal quality. For evaluation, we do a 6 channel measurement on the inner forearm, targeting multiple muscles used in wrist and finger flexion, and do an exercise with both low effort and high effort movements, to observe both individual action potentials, as well as complex MUAPs. (figure \ref{fig:emgresult})

In the frequency domain, the highest signal component is $-73\dBV$ at $110\Hz$, and the noise floor is around $-85\dBV$, for an SNR of $12\dB$ without preamplification.

\subsection*{Multi-channel ECG}

As proof-of-concept for ECG, we set up a measurement for ECG Leads I (Right Arm - Left Arm), II (Right Arm - Left Leg), III (Left Arm - Left Leg), referenced to the Right Leg through the Driven Right Leg circuit. A 5 second measurement was taken without preamplification and filtered using the MATLAB Signal Processing Toolbox \texttt{lowpass} function.

Because the ECG signal has a more restrained frequency composition, aggressive digital filtering can significantly help the signal quality. Before filtering, the noise floor lied around $-110\dBV$ and the lead II signal's peak was $-90\dBV$ at $13.5\Hz$ for an SNR of $20\dB$. After filtering, the noise floor dropped to $-160\dBV$ for an improved SNR of $70\dB$.

\subsection*{Single-channel ECG}

To demonstrate both the preamplifier and best ADC SNR, we measure a single channel ECG, using the V1 lead. The positive electrode is placed vertically between the 4th and 5th rib, $4\cm$ to the left of the sternum, and the negative electrode on the back of the patient, approximately symmetrical to the positive electrode relative to the heart. Signal quality is much better than without the preamplifier, with a signal peak of $-48\dBV$ at $2\Hz$ and noise floor at $-120\dBV$, for a digitally unprocessed SNR of $72\dB$, which is on par with high-performance commercial systems.

\section{Conclusion (1-2pg)}

We present a hardware + software solution intended for easy and affordable maintainability, using off-the-shelf components and open source hardware and software, capable of doing biopotential measurements of up to 16 single-ended or differential channels, with $>1000\Hz$ sample rates in all configurations. Differential measurements can be made directly or by use of a differential preamplifier module with a gain of $24$ and active low pass filtering ($500\Hz$).
